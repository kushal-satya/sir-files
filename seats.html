Did the revision decide any seat?

Eight close seats

In a handful of constituencies the deletions were so large relative to the margin that they could have changed the winner. The record shows they might have, in some; it does not show that they did, in any.

Seats chosen by a fixed rule before any seat-level evidence was examined. ECI results, CEO Tamil Nadu deletion counts and Form 20 booth returns.

Across the state, we find no seat-level sign that deletions were aimed at the DMK's strongholds, and seats that lost more names did not lose more votes than their own earlier trend predicts. That does not settle whether the revision tipped particular seats, because a seat is decided by its margin, and a margin of a few hundred votes can be overturned by a sliver of a deletion list.

The test is arithmetic. Suppose some share of a seat's deletions were wrongful, removing people who would have voted. If those people would have voted for the DMK front and its main rival in some ratio, there is a number of wrongful deletions that would have closed the margin. Of the 101 seats the front lost as runner-up, deletions exceeded the margin in 88. Exceeding the margin is a low bar. The demanding question is how lopsided the deleted would have had to be.

We follow every seat where, if half the deletions were wrongful and two-thirds of those people would have voted, the wrongly deleted would need to have favoured the front by no more than 55 to 45. There are seven. We add Kolathur, the Chief Minister's seat, which fails that test but drew the most attention. That rule was fixed before we looked at the seats. Since then we have estimated how many votes were actually lost per deleted name; on that evidence a lean below 55 per cent would have sufficed in 2 to 6 of the 101 seats, and comparing seats within districts, a reversing effect can be excluded in 69 of them but not in 32, among them Kolathur and most of the seats below.

Read the chart from the right. Where the dot sits far to the left of the end of the bar, a small fraction of the seat's deletions would have been enough. Kolathur is the exception among the eight: it would take about two-thirds of everyone removed to have been wrongly removed, and to lean towards the DMK, to account for the result.

Two other explanations run through these seats and are checked for each. First, the postal vote, which in Tamil Nadu now includes voters over 85 and those with disabilities voting at home and which leaned towards the front in most of these seats. Second, independents with the same name as a leading candidate, a long-standing local tactic.

The missing lists

Every verdict above is provisional for the same reason. The decisive record is the booth-by-booth list of deleted electors, and the list of those restored before the final roll. With it, each seat's question becomes direct: did the front lose more ground in the booths that lost more voters, after allowing for how those booths were already moving? The official lists sit behind a CAPTCHA on the Chief Electoral Officer's portal. An independent compilation, the India SIR Data Center, publishes part-wise counts; we have used them for Kolathur, where they reconcile exactly with the official total, and the same test can be run seat by seat. Before the poll, The Hindu examined the part-wise deleted lists for anomalies.

Some of the arithmetic here has been reported before. The statewide totals, Chennai's 35 per cent cut and Kolathur's 1.03 lakh were widely covered from December 2025, and before the poll The South First counted seats where deletions exceeded the 2021 margin. What we have not seen elsewhere is the step from "larger than the margin" to "how lopsided the deleted would have had to be", applied to the 2026 margins.
