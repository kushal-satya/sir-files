Tamil Nadu's voter-roll revision

Did roll deletions cost the DMK Kolathur?

A third of the seat's electors were struck off before the poll. The official record leans against the deletions having decided the result, but it is not sharp enough to rule that out.

Based on Form 20 results for seven elections, CEO Tamil Nadu roll and deletion counts, and Greater Chennai Corporation claims lists. Every figure is computed from public records; data and code are open.

In December 2025 the Special Intensive Revision of Tamil Nadu's electoral rolls removed 1,03,812 names from Kolathur, the Chennai seat M. K. Stalin had held since 2011. Four months later he lost it to V. S. Babu of the TVK by 8,795 votes. The deletions outnumbered the margin by about twelve to one, and many concluded that the revision had decided the contest.

We tested that claim against the official record. The cut was real and unusually deep: 35.7 per cent of the roll, the tenth-largest share of any seat in the state. Booth units that lost the most names gave the DMK no smaller share; whether they lost votes depends on how booth size is handled, with estimates between nothing and about a fifth of a vote per deleted name. Kolathur's swing was close to that of a synthetic twin cut about 30 per cent rather than 36, a comparison too coarse to resolve the margin. The record neither confirms nor excludes a decisive effect, and it cannot see the removal of particular individuals within a booth.

Deeper cuts did not bring bigger DMK losses

If deletions cost the DMK votes, the seats that lost the most electors should be the seats where its alliance fell furthest. They are not. Across the state the DMK front's vote share fell by about 14 points between 2021 and 2026, and it fell by roughly that much whether a seat lost a tenth of its roll or two-fifths of it.

The relationship is statistically indistinguishable from zero in every specification we estimated, including district fixed effects and weighting by electorate; a randomisation test gives p = 0.26. One pattern does appear. Deletions were heaviest where the TVK did best (r = 0.57). That is geography: compare seats within the same district, with urbanisation and literacy held fixed, and the deletion rate no longer predicts TVK's share. Young, mobile, renting city electorates were both harder to enumerate and more drawn to the new party.

Nor do the returns carry the fingerprints that vote manipulation usually leaves. When ballots are stuffed, turnout and the winner's share rise together, and constituencies smear towards the top right of the chart below. Tamil Nadu's 234 seats form a single cloud.

Turnout and the winner's share are, if anything, slightly negatively related (r = −0.12), and no constituency returned a winner with more than 70 per cent of the vote; this is a constituency-level screen, since booth electorates are not public. Of 27 digit tests on booth and constituency counts, seven fall below the 5 per cent level and none survives a correction for testing many at once. Kolathur itself is unremarkable: in the statewide relationship between deletions and the swing it sits close to the line.

Could deletions have tipped any seat?

Kolathur was not the only seat where deletions dwarfed the margin. The DMK front lost 101 seats as runner-up, and in 88 of them more names were deleted than votes separated the top two. But deletions change a result only if those removed would have voted, and voted differently from everyone else. For each seat we asked what share of the votes removed would have had to be the DMK's to cover the margin. The chart uses an illustrative assumption, half the deletions wrongful and two-thirds of those voters turning out; the text below uses our estimates of how many votes were actually lost per deleted name.

Our estimates put the votes lost per deleted name at no more than 0.10 to 0.28, depending on the design. On that range, a lean below 55 per cent would have been enough in only 2 to 6 of the 101 seats. Reversing Kolathur would have needed the lost votes to be 65 to 91 per cent DMK. The DMK won 75 per cent of the two-party vote here in 2021 and 47 per cent in 2026, so the requirement is far above how Kolathur voted in 2026 and, at the low end of the range, below how it voted in 2021. Comparing seats within districts, a reversing effect on the front's swing can be excluded in 69 of the 101 seats; Kolathur is not one of them.

Chennai, seat by seat

The cuts were deepest in Chennai, where every one of the sixteen seats lost between 24 and 42 per cent of its roll. Few of the deleted came back through the claims process before the final roll.

Kolathur, close up

Kolathur's roll had grown steadily since the seat was created in 2008. The revision removed a third of it in one step. Of the names struck off, 12,994 were recorded as dead, 89,251 as shifted or absent and 1,567 as enrolled elsewhere. During the claims window 16,306 people applied to be added back.

The statewide pattern does not settle Kolathur, because deletions act before a vote is cast. The calculation below makes the condition explicit. Booth by booth, at last

The test this page was built to wait for can now be run. The India SIR Data Center, an independent compilation of the CEO's part-wise deletion lists, publishes counts by reason for each of Kolathur's 286 new parts. They add up exactly to the official figure of 1,03,812, and the part numbers match the December 2025 polling-station list, which is the numbering of the 2026 Form 20. We took the counts only. Because booth electorates are not public, we scale each booth's deletions by the votes it cast in 2021, linked through the same street-level crosswalk.

If the revision cost the DMK the seat, the DMK should have fallen furthest where the most names went. Booths were renumbered in 2026, so we compare 125 stable units, groups of booths whose 2021 and 2026 votes are exact sums and need no estimation. Within the same ward, a unit one standard deviation more heavily cut saw the DMK's share move by +0.3 points (wild cluster bootstrap p = 0.36); the same comparison for the 2021 to 2024 change, before the revision, gives −0.5. Nor were the DMK's strongest booths singled out: within wards, neither the number of names removed nor the share recorded as "absent", the most discretionary code, rises with the DMK's 2021 strength.

A sharper test asks where the votes went. If the people struck off had been voting, units that lost more names should have cast fewer votes in 2026 than in 2021: about 0.65 fewer for every deletion at a normal turnout. The answer depends on how size is handled, because a unit's deletions track its size almost exactly. In raw counts the change is +0.03 votes per deleted name; per part it is −0.13 (95 per cent interval −0.19 to +0.06). Somewhere between nothing and about a fifth of a vote per deleted name, well short of 0.65, but not nothing for certain.

That is a comparison within the seat, and Kolathur's cut was nearly even across its booths, so an effect of the whole cut on every booth would not show in it. The check on the whole seat is a synthetic Kolathur: a weighted blend of other constituencies matched on urbanisation, literacy, caste composition, 2021 vote, earlier swing, roll growth and turnout, without using deletions. Kolathur lost 5.9 points more of its roll than its twin. Its swing against the DMK front differed from the twin's by 0.2 points, about 380 votes, an unremarkable gap by the standard of every other seat's twin, though the twin was itself cut about 30 per cent, and placebo gaps are large enough that this comparison could not reliably detect an effect the size of the margin.

What this cannot rule out: a voter deleted in one booth who re-enrolled in another is invisible to a comparison of booths, and selective removal of particular people within a booth would need the name-level lists. The record neither confirms nor excludes a decisive effect of the deletions in Kolathur; what evidence there is leans against one.
